IT risk fails more often from **unclear ownership** than from weak tools. Everyone assumes someone else is watching the risk, and nobody is. The Three Lines Model — updated by the IIA in 2020 — is the most useful tool we have for fixing that.

## The model, in one picture

```
First line   →  Own and manage risk      (business & IT operations)
Second line  →  Guide, monitor, challenge (risk, security, compliance)
Third line   →  Independent assurance     (internal audit)
```

(The 2020 refresh also puts **governing bodies** — board, audit committee — above the three lines, with the first line redefined to include "IT" explicitly as a risk-owning function.)

## First line: the people who own the risk

These are the teams that **run the business and IT** — application owners, infrastructure, engineering, operations. They *take* the risk, so they *own* it.

Their job: operate controls, identify risks in their area, and act on them.

**Common failure:** treating risk as something you send *away* to the risk team. The first line generates the risk; it cannot outsource owning it.

## Second line: risk, security, compliance

The second line **designs how risk is managed**, sets standards, monitors, and challenges the first line. Security and GRC teams usually sit here.

Critical nuance: the second line **does not own the business risk**. It owns the *framework* — the register, the methodology, the reporting. It advises and challenges; it does not absolve the first line of ownership.

**Common failure:** the risk team quietly becomes the owner of every risk it documents, then gets blamed when nothing changes. If the second line owns the action, the first line has disengaged.

## Third line: internal audit

Internal audit provides **independent assurance** to the board that the first and second lines are actually working. It reports to the governing body, not to management, precisely so it can say uncomfortable things.

**Common failure:** audit pulled into operational work, eroding independence — or audit grading the same risks the second line already grades, producing duplicate, competing scores.

## Why lines get blurred

1. **Small teams.** One person wears three hats. Fine in a startup — but say so explicitly, and be honest that independence is limited.
2. **Responsibility vacuum.** When nobody owns a risk, the second line absorbs it by default.
3. **Confusing "manages" with "owns".** The risk team *manages the process*; the business *owns the outcome*.
4. **Assurance overlap.** Second and third lines producing separate risk scores nobody reconciles.

## Getting it right

- **Name a single owner** for every material risk — someone with authority and budget.
- **Keep the second line advisory.** It builds the method; it does not accept the risk.
- **Preserve audit independence.** The third line assures; it does not run controls.
- **Document the seams** between lines, especially in small teams, so the blur is a deliberate choice not an accident.
- **Report upward cleanly.** The board wants: top risks, ownership, trend, and what changed — not a 300-row register.

## Key takeaways

- **First line owns risk, second line guides and challenges, third line assures.**
- The risk/security function **does not own business risk**; it owns the framework.
- **Independence of the third line is not negotiable.**
- Most breakage is **blurred ownership**, not missing tools — fix names and seams first.
