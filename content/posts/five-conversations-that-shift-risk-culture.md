We established that [risk culture is the lived answer](/posts/building-a-risk-culture/) to *"will I be listened to, and what happens if I speak?"* But culture is abstract until you get concrete. It is built in **specific conversations**, repeated until they become normal.

Here are five that move the needle — and how to run each.

## 1. "What would have to be true for this to fail?"

**When:** at the start of a project or a significant change.
**What it shifts:** from optimism bias to pre-mortem thinking.

Not "what could go wrong?" (which invites performative answers) but a **pre-mortem**: *assume it has already failed — write the story of why.*

- Ask each person to write 3 failure causes, silently, then share.
- Capture them as risks with owners, right then.
- Reward the person who names the sharpest failure, not the one who reassures.

This normalises speaking about failure *before* it is costly. It also produces genuinely better risk identification than a generic brainstorm.

## 2. "Who owns this — and what do they need to do it?"

**When:** the moment a risk is identified.
**What it shifts:** from vague accountability to real ownership.

The trap is assigning risk to teams ("IT", "the business") or to people with no authority. Ask out loud:

- **Who is the single accountable human?** (Not a group.)
- **Do they have the authority and budget** to act?
- **Did they agree?** ("I've put you down as owner" is not consent.)

Naming ownership in the room, with consent, removes the ambiguity where culture goes to die. It also surfaces when you are about to assign a risk to someone who cannot fix it — a signal that the *real* owner is more senior.

## 3. "Tell me what worries you."

**When:** regularly, by leaders, and then *act on it.*
**What it shifts:** psychological safety — the willingness to bring bad news early.

This is the highest-leverage conversation and the easiest to fake. The rules:

- **Ask genuinely**, not as a formality, and then **stay quiet.**
- **Thank the messenger**, explicitly, especially for bad news.
- **Do something visible** with what you hear — or explain why not.

One "tell me what worries you" that produces real action outweighs a year of awareness campaigns. One that produces nothing teaches everyone to stop answering.

## 4. "What did we learn?"

**When:** after an incident or a near-miss.
**What it shifts:** from blame to learning.

Near-misses are free intelligence; most organisations bury them. Run the review blamelessly but not *accountability-free*:

- **Focus on the system**, not the person: what made the error easy to make?
- **Ask "what surprised us?"** — surprises are where controls are wrong.
- **Convert output into a change** (a control, a standard, a checklist) with an owner.
- **Separate honest error from wilful disregard.** Learning culture, not a free pass.

Reward the *reporting*, even when the underlying situation is bad. The alternative is discovery by customer or auditor.

## 5. "If this were your money, would you spend it here?"

**When:** making a risk-treatment or investment decision.
**What it shifts:** from risk-speak to business framing.

Risk teams often argue in their own language ("high residual risk", "control gap"). This question reframes to what decision-makers actually weigh: **value for money.**

- If the cost of the control exceeds the expected loss, that is a real argument for **accepting** the risk.
- If it is cheap insurance against a catastrophic tail, that is a real argument for **spending**.
- Either way, it invites a business trade-off rather than a compliance "yes".

This is where [quantification and appetite](/posts/kris-that-predict/) meet culture: it makes risk a shared decision instead of a department's veto.

## Why conversations, not campaigns

Training modules change knowledge. Conversations change **norms** — what feels normal, safe, and expected. The five above cover the four levers: surfacing risk (1, 3), owning it (2), learning from it (4), and deciding on it in business terms (5).

Run them consistently, and they stop being "risk conversations" and become just how the organisation works. That is culture.

## Key takeaways

- Culture shifts through **specific, repeatable conversations**, not campaigns.
- **Pre-mortem** to surface risk; **name a single owner with authority**; **ask what worries people** and act.
- **After-action reviews** turn incidents and near-misses into controls — blame the system, not just the person.
- **"If this were your money…"** moves risk decisions into business language.
- Consistency is the mechanism: norms form when these become ordinary.
